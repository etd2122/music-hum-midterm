Exam format

Part I · Composers & Titles

Identify composer and title from the playlist recordings. You may or may not get a follow-up question, drawn from the underlined terms on the slides. Chávez: only the first 4:30 of the track.

Part II · Reading ID

Identify short excerpts by the source titles listed. Unlike the midterm, no context explanation.

Part III · Music-Text Analysis

An unfamiliar song + its text, played 4 times. Write an essay on how the composer brings the text to musical life, using musical elements as evidence.

Follow-ups come from the underlined slide terms. Each card lists them, and all of them are collected in Underlined terms. The “Lecture clip” links are the YouTube videos from the slides; final run-throughs should be on the playlist.

Part III · Music-text analysis

You hear the song four times. Use each hearing for a different job, and tie every musical observation to a specific word, line or idea in the text.

A plan for the four hearings

  1. Before hearing 1: read the text. Mark the speaker, the mood, the turning point, and vivid images (motion, nature, emotions, death, love).
  2. Hearing 1, big picture: genre & forces (voice + piano? orchestra?), overall mood, form (strophic/repeating verses vs. through-composed), where the climax is.
  3. Hearing 2, melody & text-setting: syllabic vs. melismatic, high/low register on key words, conjunct vs. disjunct, word painting.
  4. Hearing 3, accompaniment & harmony: what the piano/orchestra depicts, consonance vs. dissonance, major vs. minor shifts, ostinatos, texture changes.
  5. Hearing 4, confirm & time-stamp: tempo & dynamics changes, the ending (resolved or not?), and grab 2–3 precise moments as evidence.

Elements checklist: ask of every line

Essay structure

  1. Intro / thesis: name the text’s central idea and claim how the music shapes it (e.g. “The composer turns a calm poem into a growing nightmare through an accompaniment that…”).
  2. Body 1–3: one musical strategy per paragraph (e.g. accompaniment as image; melody & text-setting; harmony & form), each with a specific moment in the text and what you hear there.
  3. Turning point: show how the music changes when the text changes.
  4. Ending: how the final bars resolve (or don’t) and what that says about the text.
  5. Use course vocabulary and compare briefly to pieces we studied (Erlkönig, Im wunderschönen Monat Mai, Schwanenlied, Deep River, Strange Fruit…).
Practice: try the four-hearing routine on art songs not on our list, e.g. Schubert’s “Gretchen am Spinnrade” or “Der Lindenbaum,” or Robert Schumann’s “Ich grolle nicht.” Find the text and a recording, set a timer, and write a 4-paragraph essay.
